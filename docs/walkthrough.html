Part I — The concepts

A causal question is not a prediction question

Prediction asks "given what I see, what is Y likely to be?" Causal inference asks "if I intervene and change T, how would Y change?" The whole difference is the word intervene. A model can correctly learn that people carrying lighters get more lung cancer — yet confiscating lighters would do nothing, because smoking causes both. Prediction exploits associations; causation requires you to know why the association exists.

The shift in notation We don't want E[Y | T=1] — the average outcome among those who happen to be treated (an observation). We want E[Y | do(T=1)] — the average outcome if we set everyone's treatment to 1 (an intervention). Causal inference is the discipline of recovering the second from data that only contains the first.

Potential outcomes & the fundamental problem

Each unit i has two potential outcomes: Yᵢ(1), what would happen if treated, and Yᵢ(0), what would happen if not. The causal effect for that unit is simply the difference:

causal effect for unit i = Yᵢ(1) − Yᵢ(0)

The catch — the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference — is that you only ever observe one of them. A manager either took the program or didn't; her other timeline is gone forever. The value we'd need is a counterfactual, and it is missing data, permanently.

The whole game Because individual effects are unknowable, we estimate averages across groups — filling each unit's missing cell using the other group. That swap is only valid if the groups are comparable. Every method in this primer is a different way of making them comparable.

Why the naive comparison lies: confounding & selection

The tempting estimate is the difference in observed averages, E[Y|T=1] − E[Y|T=0]. Algebraically it equals the thing you want plus a poison term:

naive difference = ATT + ( E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0] ) └ true effect ┘ └────────── selection bias ──────────┘

The selection-bias term asks: even untreated, would the treated group have differed from the controls? Whenever a common cause pushes units toward treatment and toward higher outcomes, the answer is yes. That common cause is a confounder.

A 30-second numeric demonstration

Suppose motivated managers are both more likely to enroll and higher-scoring regardless of training. Within each motivation level the true effect is +0.3 — but the pooled numbers look far bigger, because the treated pool is stuffed with high-motivation people:

Choosing an estimand: which average effect?

"The effect" is ambiguous until you say for whom. Picking the estimand is a decision about the question, made before any modeling.

ATE — Average Treatment Effect

E[Y(1)−Y(0)] over everyone. The effect of a universal roll-out. "What if we made it mandatory?"

ATT — …on the Treated

E[Y(1)−Y(0) | T=1] over those who actually took it. "Did it help the people who opt in?" The right call for a voluntary program.

CATE — Conditional ATE

E[Y(1)−Y(0) | X=x] for a subgroup. "For whom does it work best?" The basis of targeting and heterogeneity analysis.

LATE — Local ATE

The effect for compliers — units an instrument nudges into treatment. What instrumental-variable designs recover; not the same as ATE or ATT.

Why it matters: these diverge when effects are heterogeneous. A strongly positive ATT does not promise the program helps the reluctant majority (ATE). Quoting the wrong one is a silent error no diagnostic will catch.

DAGs: the grammar of causal assumptions

A Directed Acyclic Graph draws your beliefs about what causes what; arrows are direct causal effects. Its power is mechanical — the graph's structure tells you which variables you must adjust for, which to leave alone, and whether the effect is recoverable at all. Three building blocks decide everything.

Fork — a confounder

T ← X → Y. X creates a fake association (dashed). A backdoor path is open.

✓ ADJUST for X

Chain — a mediator

T → M → Y. M carries the effect. Adjusting for it deletes part of what you're measuring.

✗ do NOT adjust (for the total effect)

Collider

T → C ← Y. The path is closed by default. Conditioning on C opens a spurious one.

✗ do NOT adjust (it creates bias)

The backdoor criterion (the punchline)

A "backdoor path" is any path from T to Y that begins with an arrow into T — these carry confounding. An adjustment set Z identifies the causal effect if it (1) blocks every backdoor path and (2) contains no colliders or descendants of T. Get Z right and a simple formula returns the causal effect:

E[Y | do(T=1)] = Σz E[Y | T=1, Z=z] · P(Z=z) # "adjust, then average over Z"
The most common own-goal People throw every available variable into the model. But adjusting for a mediator hides the effect, and adjusting for a collider manufactures bias from nothing. More controls is not safer — the DAG, not data availability, decides what goes in.

Identification strategies: buying an assumption you can defend

If a complete set of confounders were always observed, life would be easy. It rarely is — motivation, ability, and health go unmeasured. Each identification strategy is a different trick for handling the confounding you can't see, and each one buys a key untestable assumption. Choosing a strategy is choosing which assumption you're willing to defend.

The mental model There is no assumption-free causal estimate. You always trade an untestable belief for an answer. Good practice isn't avoiding assumptions — it's choosing ones that are plausible for your setting and being loud about them.

The estimator toolbox (for a backdoor design)

Once you've decided what to adjust for, several estimators turn that into a number. They fail in different ways — which is the whole reason to run more than one.

1 · Outcome regression / ANCOVA

Model the outcome — Y ~ T + X — and read the coefficient on T (or predict both potential outcomes per unit and average the gap). Relies on getting the outcome model roughly right; breaks if the Y–X relationship is badly nonlinear.

2 · The propensity score — one number instead of many

The propensity score e(X) = P(T=1 | X) is each unit's probability of being treated given its covariates. Rosenbaum & Rubin proved a small miracle: if adjusting for all of X removes confounding, then adjusting for the single number e(X) does too. It collapses a high-dimensional balancing problem into one dimension you can match, stratify, or weight on.

3 · Inverse-probability weighting (IPW)

Reweight units to build a pseudo-population in which treatment looks random. For the ATT, treated units get weight 1 and each control is weighted by e(X)/(1−e(X)) — controls that look like treated managers count for more, standing in for the missing counterfactuals.

ATTIPW = mean Y over treated − weighted mean Y over controls # control weight = e/(1−e)

Relies on getting the propensity model right; breaks when a few weights explode — a positivity problem (next section).

4 · Doubly-robust / AIPW — two shots at being right

Augmented IPW fuses an outcome model and a propensity model so the estimate is consistent if either one is correctly specified — and you don't have to know which. That's "double robustness," and it's why AIPW is the primary estimator in Part III.

outcome model μ(X)＋ propensity e(X)→ AIPW→ unbiased if either is right
Why run all four? If the naive number is wild but ANCOVA, IPW, and AIPW agree with each other, the estimate isn't an artifact of one modeling choice. Convergence across methods that fail differently is real evidence.

The four assumptions every backdoor estimate rests on

1 · Conditional exchangeability mostly untestable

Given X, treatment is as-good-as-random: Y(0),Y(1) ⟂ T | X — i.e. no unmeasured confounder. The big one. You defend it with domain knowledge and probe it with sensitivity analysis; data alone can't confirm it.

2 · Positivity / overlap testable

Every kind of unit could have gone either way: 0 < e(X) < 1. If some stratum is always treated, there's no control to compare against — no model invents the missing counterfactual. Checked directly from the propensity distribution.

3 · Consistency definitional

The treatment is well-defined and the observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the treatment actually received. "The program" must mean one coherent thing.

4 · SUTVA / no interference substantive

One unit's treatment doesn't spill onto another's outcome. Plausible for individual training; not for vaccines or strong network effects.

Did it work? Diagnostics, refutation, and sensitivity

An estimate is only as good as the checks it survives. Three families, each answering a different worry.

Diagnostics — "is my adjustment doing its job?"

  • Overlap: plot the propensity distributions. Treated regions must have control mass beneath them, or there is nothing to compare against.
  • Balance: after weighting/matching, covariates should look the same in both groups. The standardized mean difference (SMD) measures the gap in standard-deviation units; the convention is |SMD| < 0.1. This is the payoff check — it shows the confounders are no longer lopsided.

Refutation — "can I break it?"

  • Placebo / negative control: run the pipeline on something that cannot have an effect (a shuffled treatment, or a pre-treatment outcome). It should return ≈0. If it finds an "effect," the method is hallucinating.
  • Robustness: re-estimate on the overlap-only sample, swap learners, try alternate adjustment sets. Big swings mean fragility.

Sensitivity — "how wrong could my untestable assumption be?"

Exchangeability can't be tested, but you can ask how strong a hidden confounder would have to be to overturn the result. The E-value answers exactly that: the minimum association (on a risk-ratio scale) an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain the estimate away. A large E-value means only an implausibly strong hidden cause could be the culprit; a small one means the finding is fragile.

Calibrated language The conclusion's wording should track this evidence. Clean assumptions + clean diagnostics + clean refutation → "estimated causal effect." A concern or two → "consistent with a causal effect." Weak design or failed checks → "an association, not robust to…." Never write "X causes Y" when the assumptions are merely hopeful.
Part II — How this repo turns the concepts into a process

What this repository enforces

The repo is not a library; it's a discipline. It hard-codes the order of operations above so the thinking happens before the coding, every time. Three pieces:

📋 configs/project.yaml

The single source of truth: intervention, who was treated, outcomes, covariates, data paths. Read first; never re-asked.

📚 the causal-inference skill

The 10-step methodology plus reference files (design playbook, DAGs, diagnostics, refutation). When it conflicts with intuition, it wins.

🤖 the agents

Planner, Implementer, Reviewer, Orchestrator — each owns a slice and hands off only when guardrails pass.

Part III — Seeing it all on one problem

The worked example: a voluntary leadership program

L&D ran a 3-month leadership program (Jan–Mar) to lift manager effectiveness and cut turnover. Participation was voluntary, and some departments promoted it harder than others — a textbook observational setup with selection baked in.

Treatment

Entered the program. 570 of 9,000 (6.3%).

Outcomes (June, 1–5)

Manager Efficacy (primary), Stay Intention, Workload. Prior-year baselines available.

The confounder

Latent manager motivation drives both who enrolls and who scores well.

Why synthetic data with a known answer The dataset is generated with a true effect baked in — Efficacy +0.30, Stay +0.25, Workload +0.15. Real studies never reveal the truth, so you can't tell a good method from a lucky one. Here we can grade the workflow against the answer key.

Design & DAG — applying §DAGs and §Identification

Estimand: the ATT — voluntary uptake makes "did it help those who attend?" the honest question. Strategy: backdoor adjustment, because the major drivers of enrollment (performance, tenure, baseline scores, departmental push) are measured. The unmeasured piece — pure motivation — is the named residual threat (the partly-unobserved Q below).

Manager motivation (Q, partially UNOBSERVED) │ ╲ ▼ ▼ performance, Enrollment (T) ─────────► Efficacy_followup (Y) baseline scores ◄──┐ ▲ ▲ │ │ │ │ └────────────┘ dept_promo_intensity │ │ baseline_efficacy ──────────────────(direct path)─────┘

Adjust for (block the backdoors): baseline indices, performance, tenure, team size, span of control, demographics, org/region/job family, dept promotion. Excluded as post-treatment (would act as mediators/colliders): all follow-up outcomes and retention flags.

Identification strategy: backdoor adjustment (conditional exchangeability)
Required assumptions:
  1. Conditional exchangeability given the adjustment set   — IDENTIFYING, partly untestable
  2. Positivity for the treated                             — TESTABLE (diagnostics)
  3. Consistency · 4. SUTVA                                 — substantive
Identification status: identified  (conditional on assumption 1)

Estimator: AIPW (doubly robust) primary; ANCOVA and IPW as cross-checks; the naive difference shown only to expose the bias.

The bias decomposition — this is the lesson

The efficacy estimate walks toward truth in two moves: one the data can make, one it can't.

naive +1.03 ──(adjust for measured confounders)──► adjusted +0.42 ──(unmeasured Q)──► truth +0.30 └─── selection bias we CAN remove ≈ 0.61 ───┘ └ residual we CANNOT ≈ 0.12 ┘

Selection bias is huge

Naive efficacy is 3× the truth — motivated managers opt in. Adjustment removes ~75% of the gap.

Confounding flips signs

Naive workload looks negative; adjusted, it's correctly positive. The raw number was backwards.

Adjustment isn't magic

The estimate still sits above truth — the residual is exactly the unmeasured confounder the DAG warned about.

Diagnostics & refutation — applying §Trust

Diagnostics

PASS Balance: max |SMD| after IPW = 0.023 (< 0.10). Performance rating went from SMD 1.10 → 0.005 — the confounder is no longer lopsided.

PASS ATT positivity: only 0.35% of treated lie outside control support; 0% have e(X) > 0.98.

~41% of controls sit at e(X) < 0.02 — expected for a rare program and harmless for ATT (they carry ≈0 weight). The generic "<5% extreme PS" rule is the wrong guardrail here; the design uses the treated-support check instead — a concept-driven correction, not a rule-of-thumb.

Refutation & sensitivity

PASS Placebo (shuffled treatment): ATT = −0.028 ≈ 0. The pipeline doesn't invent effects.

STABLE Trim-to-overlap: ATT = 0.416, unchanged.

MODERATE E-value = 2.45 (CI bound 2.27): a hidden confounder would need a ~2.4× association with both enrollment and efficacy to erase the estimate. Motivation is plausible at moderate strength — consistent with the small residual bias above.

Interpretation — calibrated to the evidence

The honest sentence The results are consistent with a positive causal effect of the program on participating managers: roughly +0.3 to +0.4 points on Manager Efficacy and ~+0.3 on Stay Intention (1–5 scale), for the kind of manager who chooses to attend (the ATT).

Why "consistent with" and not "the program caused"? Diagnostics and refutation are clean, but the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption can't be verified and the E-value rates robustness as moderate — so we land on the middle tier of the calibration ladder from §Trust. Decision: the evidence supports continuing the program; before scaling, corroborate with behavioral retention and add randomized encouragement so the next study can lean on an instrument instead of exchangeability.
